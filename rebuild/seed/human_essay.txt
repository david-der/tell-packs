I didn't plan to spend my Saturday rebuilding the fence. But the neighbor's dog got through again, and my wife had opinions, so there I was at the hardware store at 7am buying cedar pickets I couldn't really afford.

The old fence came down easier than expected. Forty years of Michigan winters had done most of the work already; half the posts snapped off at ground level with one good shove. What I hadn't counted on was the concrete. Whoever set those posts in 1985 believed in concrete the way some people believe in God. Big, sloppy, forty-pound blobs of it, and every single one had to come out.

By noon I'd dug out three. My back hurt. My neighbor Ted wandered over around one, beer in hand, and stood there watching me swing a pickaxe for a while before offering the single most useless piece of advice I've ever received: "You know they make machines for that."

Thanks, Ted.

The thing about this kind of work is that it gives you time to think, and thinking is exactly what I'd been avoiding all month. The layoffs at the plant. Dad's diagnosis. The way my daughter has stopped telling me things, not out of anger, just drift. You can't fix any of that with a pickaxe. You can, however, hit a lump of 1985 concrete really hard, which turns out to be almost as good.

By dusk I had six posts set and plumb. The pickets can wait until next weekend. When I came inside, dirt in every crease of my hands, my wife looked up and asked how it went. Fine, I said. It went fine. And weirdly, for the first time in weeks, that felt true.
